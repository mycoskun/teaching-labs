Two identical cars meet the same hill

The orange car uses a fixed throttle (open loop). The blue car measures its speed and corrects the error (closed loop). Both are set to 90 km/h.

Predict first, then try

  1. Before pressing Start 3 % hill: how much speed will each car lose? Write a number down.
  2. Set the hill back to 0 and add +30 % drag. Which car notices?
  3. Set K = 0. What does the blue car turn into?
  4. Turn on the engine lag, then raise K step by step. Where does the blue car start to oscillate, and where does the oscillation grow?
Model and numbers

Point-mass car with linear drag, as in the Lecture 1 enriched slides (Part 3):

m dv/dt = −b v + F − m g · grade

m = 1000 kg, b = 50 N·s/m (nominal), g = 9.81 m/s², set speed r = 25 m/s = 90 km/h. Open loop: F = b r (calibrated for flat road and nominal drag). Closed loop: F = b r + K (r − c). With lag: the engine force follows the command with a 1 s first-order lag, and the speed measurement is 0.4 s old.

Hand check for a 3 % hill, no lag: open loop loses m g (0.03) / b = 294.3 / 50 = 5.9 m/s = 21.2 km/h. Closed loop with K = 500 loses 294.3 / 550 = 0.54 m/s = 1.9 km/h. The error shrinks by (b + K)/b = 11.

With lag and delay the loop becomes unstable above K ≈ 2800 N·s/m (phase crossover at 1.53 rad/s for K e−0.4s / ((s + 1)(1000 s + 50))). You will learn to compute such limits in Lecture 8.

This is a teaching model. A linear drag of 50 N·s/m gives 1250 N at 90 km/h, roughly three times the rolling and air resistance of a real car, so the open-loop losses here are illustrations, not predictions for a real car.

Parameters m = 1000 kg, b = 50 N·s/m: University of Michigan, Control Tutorials for MATLAB and Simulink, Cruise Control: System Modeling (ctms.engin.umich.edu). A nonlinear version with engine map and gears: K. J. Åström, R. M. Murray, Feedback Systems, 2nd ed. (2021), §4.1. Same model as make_figs_L01.py.

Predict first, then try

  1. Press Step into the shower with the default impatience. Does the temperature overshoot 38 °C?
  2. Raise the impatience until the oscillation stops dying out. Compare aL with π/2 = 1.57.
  3. Keep the impatience fixed and lower the flow. Why does the same hand now oscillate?
  4. Switch to You and steer the valve yourself. What strategy works: fast or patient?
Model and numbers

Tmix(t) = (qh Th + qc Tc) / (qh + qc)   Tskin(t) = Tmix(t − L)   dx/dt = −κ (Tskin − 38 °C)

x is the hot share of the valve. Th = 55 °C, Tc = 12 °C (assumed values: WHO asks for stored hot water at 60 °C or more and at least 50 °C at the tap; mains cold water depends on season and region). Pipe: 2 m of 15 mm copper (13.6 mm bore) plus a 1.5 m hose of 8 mm bore holds 0.37 L, so L = 3.7 s at 6 L/min and 1.8 s at 12 L/min. The flush cuts the cold flow by 40 % for 8 s.

Near the set point the loop is a pure delay plus an integrator, with loop gain a = κ (Th − Tc). It settles without oscillation for aL < 1/e, with a decaying oscillation for 1/e < aL < π/2, and the oscillation grows for aL > π/2. At the limit the period is 4L.

Sources: shower model after R. H. Gielen, PhD thesis, TU Eindhoven (2013), Example 1.1, doi:10.6100/IR747896; stability limit aL < π/2: N. D. Hayes, J. London Math. Soc. 25 (1950) 226–232; the three regimes: C. Budd, “The shower equation”, Plus Magazine (2021), plus.maths.org; hot-water temperatures: WHO, Legionella and the prevention of legionellosis (2007), §4.4.1.

The flyballs measure the speed and move the steam valve

When the load grows, the engine slows, the balls drop inwards and the linkage opens the valve. The speed recovers, but not all the way: Watt's governor is a proportional controller.

Disturbance w: the machines driven by the engine.
Damping of the arm motion. Vyshnegradskii (1876): without enough friction the governor “hunts”.
68.1
steady speed at this load [rpm]
2.9
b J ω₀ / (2 m F), stable if > 1

Predict first, then try

  1. Press Add 20 % load. Will the speed come back to 68 rpm? Read the steady speed.
  2. Lower the friction to about 1.6 kg/s and repeat. The governor is calm at the old load. What happens after the step?
  3. Lower the friction to 1.0 kg/s. This oscillation is the “hunting” that Maxwell analysed in 1868.
  4. Why is a controller that leaves a speed error still useful? What would remove the error? (Lecture 7: integral action.)
Model and numbers

φ̈ = ω² sin φ cos φ − (g/l) sin φ − (b/m) φ̇   J ω̇ = k cos φ − F

φ is the arm angle from the spindle, ω the engine (and spindle) speed, k cos φ the steam torque admitted by the valve linkage, F the load torque. Equilibrium: cos φ₀ = F/k and ω₀² = g k / (l F), so a larger load gives a lower steady speed (droop). Stability condition (Vyshnegradskii, in Pontryagin's form): b J ω₀ / (2 m F) > 1.

Illustrative parameters, not data of a real engine: m = 1 kg, l = 0.3 m, J = 20 kg·m², F₀ = 100 N·m, k = 155.6 N·m (φ₀ = 50°), so ω₀ = 7.13 rad/s = 68.1 rpm and the critical friction is 1.40 kg/s at F₀ and 1.84 kg/s at 1.2 F₀. The arms stop at 5° and 85°.

Sources: L. S. Pontryagin, Ordinary Differential Equations (1962), as given by J. Sotomayor, L. F. Mello, D. C. Braga, arXiv:math/0604177, and by R. Ball, P. Holmes, arXiv:nlin/0702044; M. Denny, “Watt steam governor stability”, Eur. J. Phys. 23 (2002) 339–349; J. C. Maxwell, “On governors”, Proc. R. Soc. Lond. 16 (1868) 270–283.

Try this order

  1. Eyes open twice. This is your closed-loop score.
  2. Eyes closed: the green dot disappears after the first second. You still see the target. Why does your error grow even though you know where the target is?
  3. Delayed view: you see the green dot 0.6 s late, like hot water still in the pipe. Try moving fast, then try moving calmly.
  4. Unstable: the dot runs away from your hand, like a stick balanced on your palm. You must keep catching it.
Model and numbers

Positions are fractions of the track width. Eyes open, eyes closed and delayed view: τ dc/dt = u + w − c with τ = 0.25 s. Unstable: dc/dt = λ (c − u) + 0.3 w with λ = 1.2 s⁻¹, so the dot runs away from your hand unless you keep correcting. The target r is a sum of three slow sine waves (0.09–0.37 Hz), the disturbance w a sum of two. Score: root-mean-square of r − c over the 20 s run.

You are not an instant controller. Your simple visual reaction time is about 0.2 s (mean 231 ms in 1469 adults, Woods et al. 2015), and your screen and browser add a few tens of milliseconds. In continuous tracking, people adapt so that they and the machine together behave like an integrator with an effective delay of roughly 0.1–0.3 s (the crossover model of McRuer and Jex, 1967). The delay is why a very short stick cannot be balanced on the palm: the shortest balanceable stick grows with the square of the delay (Kovacs, Milton and Insperger, 2019).

Milton, T. Insperger, R. Soc. Open Sci. 6 (2019) 191006, doi:10.1098/rsos.191006; balancing on one foot with eyes closed: Åström and Murray, Feedback Systems, 1st ed., Exercise 1.3.
