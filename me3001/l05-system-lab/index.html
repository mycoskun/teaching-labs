Predict first

Double the inertia J. What happens to the final speed, and to the time the motor needs to get there?

The final speed e/k2 stays the same, because J does not appear in it. The time constant τ = JR/(k1k2) doubles, so the motor needs twice as long. Try it with the slider.

Model and equations

Armature circuit (Kirchhoff) and shaft (Newton), slides 12 and 13:

e − eb = R i + L didt,   eb = k2ω,   J dωdt = k1i

With L neglected (slide 14):

ω(s)E(s) = 1/k2τs + 1,   τ = JRk1k2

Keeping L gives a second-order model, ω/E = k1/(LJs2 + RJs + k1k2). Its electrical time constant L/R is usually far shorter than τ, which is why the lecture neglects L. The sheet uses k1 = k2 = k (SI units) and no friction, as in the lecture.

The suspension trades ride comfort against grip

Example 2, Figs. 5.4 and 5.5, slides 15 to 21
How to use this sheetclick to fold
  1. Choose a road above the sliders: an 8 cm bump, a 5 cm kerb or a wavy road. Set the driving speed V.
  2. The drawing shows the two models of the lecture side by side, one mass on the left (Fig. 5.4) and two masses on the right (Fig. 5.5). Both drive over the same road. Play runs it in slow motion; vertical motion is drawn 6 times larger.
  3. Change the car with the sliders. Watch two plots: body acceleration stands for ride comfort (smaller is better) and tyre stretch stands for grip. When the tyre stretch crosses the red dashed line, a real wheel would leave the road; Results then says that grip is lost.
  4. The road-to-body gain plot shows how much of a road wave reaches the body at each frequency. Its peaks sit at the body frequency and the wheel-hop frequency listed under Results.
  5. The damping sweep repeats the whole run for 22 values of b. A point further down means more comfort, a point further left means better grip; the red dot is your b.

Try thisChoose the wavy road and lower V to 15 km/h. The road now shakes the car near its body frequency and the body moves about twice as far as the road. At 60 km/h it moves less than a quarter as far.

Predict first

Make the damper much harder (large b). Does the body shake less over the bump?

No, it shakes more. A hard damper passes the road almost straight into the body, so the peak body acceleration grows with b. A very soft damper gives the gentlest ride but lets the wheel bounce, and the tyre stretch grows again. The sweep shows it: comfort wants a small b, grip is best at a middle value. Choosing b is a trade-off between the two.

Model and equations

Two masses, all displacements up (slide 21):

m ẍa = −k(xa − xt) − b(ẋa − ẋt)
mtẍt = k(xa − xt) + b(ẋa − ẋt) − kl(xt − x)

One mass (Fig. 5.4, slide 18): one spring stands for the suspension spring and the tyre in series, keq = k kl/(k + kl), so Xa/X = (bs + keq)/(ms2 + bs + keq). Two masses (the equations of slide 21 solved together): Xa/X = kl(bs + k)/Δ(s).

The tyre is a linear spring in the model, so it can pull the wheel down. When the stretch xt − x exceeds the static tyre compression (m + mt)g/kl, a real wheel would leave the road; the sheet marks this instead of modelling it. The road arrives at the speed V; the drawing shows vertical motion 6 times larger than horizontal distance.

    Predict first

    Problem 1 has ζ = 0.011. Roughly how many oscillations does the mass make before the motion has mostly died out?

    The envelope decays as e−ζωnt, so it is down to about 2 % after 4/(ζωn) ≈ 2860 s. One period is 2π/ωd ≈ 50 s, so the mass swings about 57 times. The step plot shows the first 300 s of it.

    Model and equations

    Write the denominator with a leading 1 and compare it term by term with the standard form (slides 39 and 45):

    outputinput = Ksωn2s2 + 2ζωns + ωn2

    The poles are s = −ζωn ± jωn√(1 − ζ2) for ζ < 1. Their distance from the origin is ωn; the smaller ζ, the closer they sit to the imaginary axis. The notes wrote ξ (xi) for the damping ratio in Problems 1, 2 and 4; this sheet uses ζ, as the deck does. Lecture 6 reads the step response in terms of these two numbers.

    Two disks on a flexible shaft keep speeding up and keep twisting

    How to use this sheetclick to fold

    2. Press Play. The coloured line on each disk shows its angle, and the bars show the torque from the gear and the torque in the shaft.

    Motor torque

    Results

    Predict first

    Switch to the half-second torque pulse. What do the disks do after the motor lets go?

    They keep turning at a constant mean speed, because nothing ties them to the ground, and they keep twisting against each other at ω = √(K(J1 + J2)/(J1J2)) for ever, because the model has no damping. The transfer function says the same: two poles at s = 0 and a pair on the imaginary axis.

    Model and equations

    Gear (1), lower disk (2), upper disk (3), slide 57:

    T2 = R2RT,   J2θ̈2 = T2 − K(θ2 − θ1),   J1θ̈1 = K(θ2 − θ1)
    θ1(s)T(s) = R2/Rs2(J1J2s2/K + J1 + J2)

    The factor s2 gives two poles at the origin: a constant torque makes the pair spin faster and faster. The other factor gives the oscillation of the disks against each other. The pinion inertia and bearing friction are neglected, as in the problem.
